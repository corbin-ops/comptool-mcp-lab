VII. SAME-OWNER ADJACENT PARCEL ACCESS
PROTOCOL
When a parcel appears landlocked, always check adjacent parcels before concluding. Same-owner adjacency
almost always means an easement exists — even if it's not labeled.
How to Identify Same-Owner Access
 Check adjacent parcels for identical owner name — exact match = almost certainly the same person
 
 Check for same LAST NAME on adjacent parcels — family members commonly share easements
 
 Look for a visible dirt track or clearing running toward a road — if people are getting in, an easement exists
 
 Click the 'X properties' link in the sidebar → see all parcels this owner holds highlighted in blue
 
 Zoom into each blue cluster to find which parcel connects to a road
 
 Purple = parcel you previously clicked on in the same owner's portfolio
 
 Even if the name is slightly different (trust vs. personal name), same last name = same person probable

XI. COUNTY-OWNED RIVER CORRIDORS — WATERFRONT
STILL APPLIES
Big Four Rd — County River Corridor (Hazards OFF)
[COUNTY RIVER CORRIDOR] Green Line = County Owned Strip — Waterfront Premium Still Applies
 Fig 11A: Big Four Rd area with hazards OFF. The green winding line following the river = county's river corridor
ownership strip. Arcadia Country Club visible top right. Also note: bottom area near Glovers Lake Rd shows lighter
 patchy area = timber regrowth visible even at this zoom level — early regrowth or recently timbered section.
The County River Corridor Rule
When a river corridor is owned by the county (green line on Land Insights), and a parcel borders that corridor —
it IS still waterfront. The county owns the strip of land immediately along the river, not the adjacent landowner.
But public access to the river exists, meaning the adjacent parcel owner can access and use the river. The
restriction: cannot build over the river or install a dock without a county easement. This is still a waterfront
premium situation — just note the county ownership distinction.
 Parcel boundary touches county river corridor = waterfront premium applies
 
 County owns the river strip = public access, but no permanent structures without easement
 
 No dock, no bridge, no building over the corridor without county permission
 
 Still call it waterfront in the comp analysis — just note the county ownership
 
 If the subject parcel directly borders the river (no county strip) = full waterfront

XII. DEVELOPER SUBDIVISION LOTS — PATTERN
RECOGNITION
Classic Developer Subdivision — Woods Trail Area
[DEVELOPER SUBDIVISION] Identical Lots, Same Shape/Size — One Parent Parcel Divided
 Fig 12A: County zoom showing developer subdivision between Woods Trail and Glovers Lake Rd. All parcels are
 identical size, same shape, same orientation — bowling alley lots lined up in rows. This was ONE large parent
parcel a developer bought, subdivided into individual lots, and sold off for profit. Comp bubbles: Matthew Swain
19.10ac | $4.45k ppa and 20.00ac | $5.25k ppa visible for comparison. State of Michigan parcels adjacent. Bottom
 shows timber regrowth near Glovers Lake Rd.
How to Recognize Developer Subdivisions
 All parcels same size, same shape, same orientation — mathematical uniformity
 
 Multiple 'same-name' owners in the grid (the developer sold them off individually)
 
 A main access road (Woods Trail in this case) running through or alongside
 
 Comp bubbles clustered in this area = multiple similar listings = child lots from same parent
 
 If comping one of these lots — use ONE comp from the cluster, not all of them
 
 Using multiple child lot comps from the same parent skews the valuation artificially
 
✓ Rule: When multiple comp bubbles are in the same tight area and they look like identical lots — they
are almost certainly child lots from one parent parcel. Use only ONE as your comp representative.

XIII. MARSHLAND — ALL COLORS & SHAPES
■ Marshland color varies by season, vegetation type, and water content. The identifier is SHAPE and
TEXTURE — not color.
Orange-Brown Marshland (New Color Calibration)
[ORANGE-BROWN MARSH] Different Color, Same Identifier — Irregular Shape + Fuzzy Texture
Fig 13A: Orange-brown marshland — same irregular blob shape and fuzzy texture as yellow-green marsh, different
 color. Large irregular mass center-left with characteristic snaking creek/stream cutting through. Completely
different texture from surrounding healthy dark green timber. Two green comps visible: Matthew Swain 19.10ac |
 $4.45k ppa and 20.00ac | $5.25k ppa. The marsh significantly affects buildability of nearby parcels.
Marshland Color Spectrum
The KEY identifiers for marsh are NEVER color — always shape and texture:
 IRREGULAR BLOB SHAPE — not clean round or oval, ragged organic outline
 
 FUZZY TEXTURE — not sharp clean canopy, blurry patchy appearance
 
 SNAKING CREEK/STREAM LINE — dark thin winding line cutting through
 
 DIFFERENT TEXTURE from surrounding healthy dark green timber
 
 LIGHTER than surrounding healthy canopy in most cases

XVI. GREEN TREE DOTS ON GIS MAPS
When viewing listing photos on Zillow or Redfin that include GIS/parcel maps, you may see green tree dots
overlaid on certain parcels. These most likely represent state land, national forest, or some form of
public/reserve land designation. Not something you'll see constantly. When you do see them:
 Note it as adjacent state/federal/public land if bordering the subject parcel
 
 Adjacent public land is actually a POSITIVE for recreational/hunting value context
 
 It means no future development will occur on that side — natural buffer preserved
 
 It does NOT restrict the subject parcel's use (unless the parcel itself is designated)
 
 Just note: 'Adjacent state/federal land designation visible on GIS map — positive for natural context'

XVII. YIELDING ASSETS — HAY, ORCHARDS, SPECIALTY
AG
■ Sellers of yielding assets almost always understand their land's value. Never assume they're
uninformed. Price accordingly and always flag.
A yielding asset is any parcel that generates ongoing income from the land itself — hay production, active
farming, orchards, timber harvest operations, hunting leases, etc. These are fundamentally different from
vacant land. The seller has a business running on that property and prices it accordingly.
Active Crop Farmland
Tight crop rows visible, tractor equipment, distinct field sections. Highest premium. Sellers are sophisticated
farmers or landowners who know every acre's value.
Hay/Pasture Production
Bales visible in Street View, tractor with baling equipment, well-maintained grass field. Flag as yielding — seller
knows the land produces income.
Commercial Nursery/Orchard
Very regular commercial planting rows, business name on parcel, distinct product stages visible. Specialty
premium. Corporate-level knowledge of land value.
Managed Timber Plantation
Uniform plantation rows from satellite, LLC/corporate ownership, systematic harvest cycles. These owners track
timber value professionally.
Active Tilled Cultivation
Reddish/orange tilled soil with cultivation lines — freshly worked, active planting cycle. Treat as farmland.

XVIII. FINAL COMPING MINDSET — PUTTING IT ALL
TOGETHER
★ Every piece of information you gather is one more pixel in the full picture. The more pixels you have,
the clearer the image — and the more accurate the offer.
The Information Hierarchy
Tier: APN Verification
The single most powerful tool. When you pull a comp's APN and load it in Land Insights, you run a full comp on
the comp itself. Flood, wetlands, road, shape, acreage, area — everything. This is the ground truth.
Tier: Street View
The second most powerful tool. It reveals what satellite never can: hay bales, road condition, water quality,
neighborhood character, structures hidden by trees. Always mandatory.
Tier: Satellite + Map Layers
Your broad-stroke read. Land type, water features, easements, terrain, timber quality. Toggle between multiple
satellite dates. Toggle hazards on and off.
Tier: Listing Photos + Description
Cross-reference tool. Do they match satellite? Do they match the APN location? Do they tell a consistent story?
Use them to confirm, not to lead.
Tier: Zillow + Redfin
Comp verification tools. MLS source confirmation, APN, price history, DOM, GIS parcel maps. Always check
both — they often have different information.
The Detective's Checklist — Before Every Comp Delivery
 Did I toggle hazards OFF to assess real terrain, then back ON before finishing?
 
 Did I check Street View for: road quality, structures, water quality, area character?
 
 Did I pull the APN and verify the comp location + acreage + land type?
 
 Did I check both Zillow AND Redfin for: MLS confirmation, APN, price history, GIS map?
 
 Did I identify the land type correctly: farmland / pasture / timber / cleared / mixed?
 
 Did I assess any water features via Street View — not just satellite color?
 
 Did I check for easements, power lines, pipelines, trailways, or railroad corridors?
 
 Did I check parcel shape for bowling alley / flagpole issues?
 
 Did I assess road quality and note the Street View year?
 
 Did I check for landlocked status and investigate same-owner access?
 
 Does the market value make sense against the PPA curve I observed?
 
 Is the offer range landing at approximately 50% of market value?
 
 Did I close all MCP tabs after the comp?
